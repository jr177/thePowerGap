About the book

Marriage, it turns out, is a mirror.

The world projects its power dynamics onto a marriage. In an interracial one, you can watch it happen.

The Power Gap is a first-person memoir and case study about the credibility, ownership, and leadership gap between women of color and white men. Interracial relationships make that gap visible: who is believed, who is assumed to own the property, and who is seen as the leader.

The book follows the author's marriage and divorce through four courts and draws on conversations with more than fifty interracial couples, along with legal experts, psychologists, and sociologists. It shows how institutions, from banks to courtrooms, quietly perpetuate unfairness, and what women of color can do about it.

The Appeals Court hearing.

The author's appeal hearing, one of four courts the book follows.

Video not loading? Open it in Google Drive.

The accounts went to Mr. Craig first. The accounting was ordered to follow.

Ms. Rodrigues argued her appeal without a lawyer. She filed an opening brief and a 600-page appendix. Her ex-husband, Mr. Craig, filed no brief and did not argue. On January 22, 2026, she wrote an open letter to the Sixth District Court of Appeal about the unequal treatment. As far as she knows, she is the only person ever to have written an open letter to a California appellate court.

The trial court handed Mr. Craig every community asset while promising an accounting would follow, yet it never explained how it conjured its arbitrary $208,357.99 offset. At the final hearing, Mr. Craig himself admitted it was simply “the number that the Court came up with.” The appeals court itself wrote that the record “does not disclose how the court valued each account as of the date of trial.”

On appeal, rather than holding the opposing party accountable, the appellate court shifted the burden entirely onto Ms. Rodrigues. It commanded both parties to calculate an offset using figures it arbitrarily told them to assume—numbers detached entirely from the trial court’s own orders—while never once requesting Mr. Craig’s missing exhibit. Even more egregiously, after oral argument, Ms. Rodrigues formally asked the court for permission to supplement the record if anything was missing, and the court refused.

The court then affirmed the judgment, claiming the burden of showing error rested solely on her shoulders. It did this even though Mr. Craig filed no respondent’s brief and did not appear on appeal.

This outcome reflects a stark double standard. Ms. Rodrigues—a woman of color—was held to an impossible standard, expected to supply every single page, including Mr. Craig’s missing documents and irrelevant filings, just to chase fabricated figures. Meanwhile, Mr. Craig—who is white—was granted total credibility without an expert witness, an accounting in the record, or even an appearance. Compounding this bias, the trial judge went so far as to brand Ms. Rodrigues a “looter” for money spent on basic survival and rent—deploying a loaded, predatory term deeply rooted in racialized tropes of criminality and lawlessness to criminalize a woman of color managing household expenses.

“Malingering.” Ms. Rodrigues has an auditory processing disorder and uses a real-time captioning screen in court. In November 2023 her expert testified that she passed the effort measures built into her testing to detect malingering. The trial judge kept using the word anyway, a medical slur for someone accused of faking a disability. On June 21, 2024, he told her, “I think you’re malingering today by intentionally trying to look at the screen.”

Her arguments on appeal
  • Bias. The trial court’s rulings were biased and procedurally deficient, including on disability and race (brief, p. 23).
  • Spousal support. It was wrongly denied for half the length of the marriage (p. 24).
  • Separate property. Her forensic accountant raised her $61,381 claim and traced $19,000 she brought into the marriage through three accounts. At the June 21, 2024 hearing the judge said he recalled denying it but would go back and check his order, and the money was just given to Mr. Craig (pp. 37–41).
  • The bank accounts. All of them were transferred to Mr. Craig, including about $60,000 in frozen community assets (pp. 44–51).
  • Sanctions. They were punitive and violated her due process rights, and the court gave no itemized breakdown of how the sanctions and credits produced the $208,357.99 offset (pp. 51–52).
The federal case

One letter froze her bank accounts. A federal court said the bank was allowed to.

Three months after Ms. Rodrigues filed for divorce, her husband’s attorney, Gretchen Boger, sent letters to Alliant Credit Union, Customers Bank and VIO Bank demanding that her bank accounts be frozen. Alliant and Customers Bank froze them within days. Alliant’s and Customers Bank’s accounts stayed frozen for years.

  1. Feb. 27, 2020

    Ms. Rodrigues files for divorce. A standard restraining order applies to both spouses from that day.

  2. April 2, 2020

    A domestic violence restraining order is granted. Mr. Craig leaves the home and stops paying rent, and Ms. Rodrigues is given custody of the children.

  3. May 22, 2020

    Mr. Craig’s attorney emails Ms. Rodrigues demanding she add his name to her accounts, or he will tell her banks that restraining orders apply to them.

  4. May 29, 2020

    His attorney, Gretchen Boger, writes to three of her banks—Alliant Credit Union, Customers Bank and VIO Bank—demanding the accounts be frozen. The family court was not told.

  5. June 1, 2020

    Alliant freezes her accounts, about $57,000. The credit union’s own lawyer tells Mr. Craig’s attorney, in writing, that the letter “overstated and misrepresented” the restraining order, and asks for a corrected letter.

  6. June 3, 2020

    Customers Bank freezes her account, about $240,000. Mr. Craig’s attorney replies to Alliant: “We are pleased that the accounts are frozen, and we request that they remain frozen until further notice.”

  7. June 5–10, 2020

    Mr. Craig’s attorney says he will not correct the letters unless Ms. Rodrigues signs a stipulation making the accounts community property: “Until we have a signed stipulation, we will not take any action.”

  8. June 16, 2020

    Alliant tells the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau the accounts are frozen “as required under California law” until a court orders otherwise.

  9. July 7, 2020

    Mr. Craig’s attorney offers to unfreeze the Alliant certificate only if the money is split equally between the spouses.

  10. Sept. 4, 2020

    Mr. Craig’s attorney writes: “we are willing, and are proposing, to withdraw our requests… to freeze the accounts on the following conditions: (1) A $50,000 advance of community property funds be sent from you to Nathan.”

  11. Sept. 10, 2020

    The offer becomes a $50,000 advance to each spouse ($100,000 total) plus a signed stipulation.

  12. Sept. 17, 2020

    Without a request for order, the family court orders that $100,000 advance, $50,000 to each. Her June 5 request for order had not asked for it or for control of the accounts, and the advance first appeared in Mr. Craig’s attorney’s email as the price of lifting the freeze.

  13. Feb. 14, 2021

    Ms. Rodrigues sues Alliant Credit Union in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California.

  14. June 1, 2022

    Customers Bank unfreezes her account, two years after the freeze.

  15. Nov. 7, 2022

    U.S. Magistrate Judge Donna M. Ryu rules for the credit union. The order says Alliant “properly froze the disputed accounts” on instructions from her husband, given through his attorney. The ruling rests on the account agreement, which lets any joint owner of an account instruct the bank, not on the restraining order.

  16. Nov. 21, 2022

    Alliant asks the court to make her pay its legal fees: $131,158.

  17. April 18, 2023

    The court denies the request. The fee clauses in the account agreement apply only when someone brings legal action against an account, or when the credit union sues to enforce the agreement, and Ms. Rodrigues was the one who sued. The judge wrote that Alliant’s argument “misleadingly highlights the second sentence” of the clause “while ignoring the first.”

  18. 2024

    The family court moves Alliant Credit Union, and all her other remaining accounts, into Mr. Craig’s name. About $59,000 of it came from the Alliant accounts. At the June 21, 2024 hearing her forensic accountant asked why she was being charged the full Alliant certificate balance. The court did not answer the question and later declined her expert’s adjustment.

  19. April 22, 2026

    The Court of Appeal affirms. See the appeal.

What the federal court did and did not decide

Magistrate Judge Ryu’s ruling was narrow. She found that the credit union’s account agreement let either joint owner direct a freeze, and that Alliant could act on a lawyer’s instruction. The court treated Mr. Craig as a joint owner of the two accounts that were frozen, a fact Ms. Rodrigues confirmed at the hearing. A separate $55,000 certificate was in her name alone. Alliant says it matured on May 26, 2020, three days before the attorney’s letter, and moved into the joint savings account. Ms. Rodrigues disputes that transfer, but the court declined to consider it. Ms. Rodrigues had told the court she used the accounts for electricity, day care, rent and car insurance, that after the freeze a utility bill went to collections and her credit score dropped, and that her parents stepped in to support her children.

In Ms. Rodrigues’s case, a lawyer’s letter was enough to cut off her money for years.

Public records

Unpublished

California has a long history of administrative opacity that harms vulnerable communities—from the 1851–1852 tribal treaties kept under a federal ‘Injunction of Secrecy’ until 1905, which denied Native tribes their homelands, legal recourse, and federal status by burying negotiated agreements. While state institutions today frequently adopt performative social justice measures—such as symbolic land acknowledgments and public apologies—they continue to perpetuate the very administrative secrecy that caused historical harm, shielding modern judicial and administrative outcomes from true public accountability.

Unpublished opinions disproportionately represent poor and appellants of color. The California Civil Rights Department has identified them as a barrier to justice. Because they’re so hard to find and aggregate, patterns in high-volume areas like family law, disability access, and discrimination claims go largely unexamined by researchers, advocates, and oversight bodies.

All of the appeals in my case were unpublished. I raised the access issue at oral argument and asked three times for my appeal’s opinion to be published. The court refused and offered no guidance on how to access California’s unpublished opinions.

Why This Matters

No Public Index

Unpublished decisions are excluded from official reports, non-citable as precedent, and virtually unsearchable.

Civil Rights Blindspots

The California Civil Rights Department cited unpublished opinions as a “barrier to justice,” effectively acting as an area where unequal application of the law may occur.

Shielding Misconduct

Low-scrutiny unpublished rulings allow procedural irregularities and judicial bias to bypass California Supreme Court review.

The CPRA Catch-22

All six California District Courts of Appeal require specific case numbers to fulfill California Public Records Act (CPRA) requests for unpublished decisions. Yet, the state provides no searchable system to find those case numbers, making comprehensive public oversight impossible.

How the book is organized

Three parts: the problem, the effects, the way forward.

I
Part one

Why the gap exists

How a societal power differential works, and how it plays out inside an interracial relationship, told through the author’s own story.

II
Part two

What the gap does

How it shapes the lives of women of color and of white men, in institutions and in relationships, from who owns property to the strains of interracial marriage.

III
Part three

How to close it

A close look at California’s legal history of secrecy and performative social justice, from the 1851–52 tribal treaties to today’s unpublished opinions. Conversations with experts, and practical recommendations for protecting and empowering women of color, in institutions and in private life.
